# 1. Introduction

## A. Historical Context

Acetaminophen (paracetamol) is one of the most widely used medicines in the world. Although it was first synthesized in 1878 by Harmon Northrop Morse, its clinical value remained overlooked for decades. At first, the market was dominated by more toxic alternatives such as acetanilide and phenacetin. It was not until the late 1940s that studies at Yale University showed that paracetamol is the active and safer metabolite of these earlier drugs, providing excellent analgesic effects without their severe adverse effects, such as methemoglobinemia and nephrotoxicity. This discovery led to its large-scale commercialization in 1955 under the Tylenol brand and marked an important milestone in pharmacology.

## B. Overall Reaction

The chemical synthesis of acetaminophen (N-(4-hydroxyphenyl)acetamide) is based on the acylation of 4-aminophenol [1, 2]. In this reaction, acetic anhydride is used to transfer an acyl group and convert the primary amine into a stable amide, producing acetic acid as the natural byproduct [4].

## C. Detailed Experimental Procedure

This procedure describes the synthesis of acetaminophen in water, based on the protocols of Srabovic [1] and the Royal Society of Chemistry [2].

### Phase 1: Preparation of Reactants
1. **Action:** In a 50 mL Erlenmeyer flask, suspend 1.0 g of 4-aminophenol in 9.0 mL of distilled water. Stir magnetically at room temperature.

2. **Justification:** 4-Aminophenol has low solubility in water at room temperature, so an initial heterogeneous suspension is expected [2]. Water is ideal because it does not compete strongly as a nucleophile against the amine.

### Phase 2: Acylation
3. **Action:** In a fume hood, add 1.1 mL of acetic anhydride to the flask while keeping the mixture under stirring.

4. **Justification and Chemistry:** Acetic anhydride is the acyl donor. To ensure complete conversion of 4-aminophenol, 27% excess anhydride is added relative to the theoretical amount. This excess is necessary in practice because part of the reagent is consumed upon contact with water. Chemically, the nitrogen of 4-aminophenol attacks the carbonyl carbon of acetic anhydride. The two molecules briefly form an intermediate before the structure breaks apart and releases acetic acid as a byproduct. At the end, the excess anhydride is converted into acetic acid and is easily removed during the washing step, leaving the final compound clean and formed.

### Phase 3: Progression and Primary Crystallization
6. **Action:** Continue stirring for approximately 10 to 15 minutes. After this time, remove the flask from the stirrer and place it in an ice-water bath for 15 minutes.

7. **Justification and Chemistry:** After the first minutes of reaction, the acetaminophen formed begins to precipitate. Cooling in an ice bath decreases the solubility of acetaminophen in water (down to 1.43 g/100 mL at 0 C), shifting the phase equilibrium toward crystallization [1, 2]. The byproduct, acetic acid, remains dissolved in the aqueous phase.

### Phase 4: Isolation of the Crude Product (Filtration)
8. **Action:** Filter the solid under vacuum using a Büchner funnel and a Kitasato flask. Wash the filtered product with 5 mL of ice-cold distilled water.

9. **Justification:** Vacuum filtration quickly separates the crude product from the acidic aqueous phase. Washing with ice-cold water is critical because it removes residual acetic acid and traces of 4-aminophenol without dissolving the newly crystallized acetaminophen [2].

### Phase 5: Product Purification (Recrystallization)
10. **Action:** Transfer the crude acetaminophen to a clean Erlenmeyer flask. Add a minimal amount of hot distilled water (approximately 10-15 mL at 80 C) until complete dissolution.

11. **Justification and Chemistry:** Recrystallization relies on thermal fractional crystallization. Upon heating, acetaminophen dissolves completely thanks to its high hot-water solubility (5.0 g/100 mL). Crystals that grow slowly during cooling exclude impurities from the crystal lattice [1].

12. **Action:** Allow the solution to cool slowly to room temperature and then place it in an ice bath to maximize yield. Filter again under vacuum and dry the crystals.

### Phase 6: Analysis and Characterization
13. **Action:** Dry the recrystallized product in a low-temperature oven (60 C) or under prolonged vacuum.

## 2.2 ALPB Solvation Model in xTB

The **ALPB solvation model** in the xTB library is an evolution of the generalized Born (GBSA) solvation approach. It is specifically designed to address the limitations of the traditional GBSA method, especially for organic systems and when high accuracy is required for solvation energies.

### Conceptual Basis

ALPB starts from the same principles as Born solvation theory, which treats the solvent as a polar continuous medium interacting with a polar solute. However, ALPB introduces important improvements in the definition of contact surfaces and in the energy evaluation.

1. **Improved Contact Surfaces:**
   In standard GBSA methods, the contact surface between solute and solvent is often defined using simple van der Waals radii. This can cause problems for systems with complex geometries, such as hydrogen-bonded structures, where the charge distribution is not perfectly spherical.
   ALPB uses a stricter contact-surface approximation. Instead of simple spheres, it defines a surface that represents the molecular region exposed to the solvent. This is achieved through a calculation that considers the real shape of the solute's electron cloud, making the solvent interaction physically more realistic.

2. **Born-Oppenheimer Limit:**
   The model name refers to a specific mathematical and physical correction used to ensure that the solvation energy is consistent with the fundamental principles of quantum mechanics.

3. **Generalized Gaussian Approximation:**
   To compute the polarization energy efficiently, ALPB approximates the solute electron density as a sum of Gaussian distributions, one for each atom. This allows a fast and numerically stable evaluation of the electrostatic interaction with the solvent field.

The solvation command used was: `xtb filename.xyz --opt --alpb water`

When moving to a realistic solvated system, the molecules interact with their physical environment through **intermolecular interactions** (van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonds).

**1. Alteration of Vibrational Modes:**
* **Broadening and shifting:** In the presence of solvent, the O-H (>3600 cm^-1) and N-H (>3400 cm^-1) bands shift to lower frequencies (3200-3400 cm^-1). **2. Physicochemical implications for reactant interaction:**

* **Effect on the Electrophile (Activation):** Solvation of the oxygen atoms of acetic anhydride increases polarization of the C=O bonds (C^delta+ = O^delta-). By moving electron density toward oxygen, the carbonyl carbon becomes more electrophilic, reducing the activation barrier for nucleophilic attack.
* **Effect on the Nucleophile:** If the solvent forms hydrogen bonds with the amino group of p-aminophenol, the nitrogen lone pair becomes stabilized by the solvent. For the reaction to occur, the system must spend energy desolvating this amino group before it can attack the anhydride.

# 4. Frontier Orbitals (HOMO and LUMO)

Chemical reactivity between two molecules can be analyzed through Molecular Orbital Theory. This theory states that the most important and energetically favorable initial interactions that lead to bond formation occur between the valence-shell molecular orbitals of the reactants.

## a) HOMO and LUMO Concept

Frontier orbitals are defined by the following concepts:

* **HOMO (Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital):** The highest-energy level that contains valence electrons in the molecule. Physically, these electrons are the least tightly held by the atomic nuclei and therefore are the most likely to be donated or shared. The HOMO is directly related to the electron-donating ability of the species and defines its nucleophilic character.

* **LUMO (Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital):** The lowest available empty energy level in the molecule. It is the optimal region for accepting electrons. The LUMO defines the electron-accepting ability of the molecule and establishes its electrophilic character.

In reactivity prediction, the primary interaction occurs between the HOMO of one molecule and the LUMO of the other.

## c) HOMO/LUMO Analysis of Each Reactant and Their Relation to Reactivity

**Acetic Anhydride Analysis:**
* **HOMO (Orbital 20):** Electron density is located mainly on the oxygen atoms, both the carbonyl oxygens and the central oxygen. These lobes correspond to nonbonding electron pairs perpendicular to the pi system of the carbonyls.

* **LUMO (Orbital 21):** This orbital has a strongly antibonding pi* character concentrated on the carbonyl groups (C=O). It is important to note the large size of the lobes on the carbonyl carbon atoms. This indicates that these carbons are the main electrophilic centers, so nucleophilic attack must occur there.

**Aminophenol Analysis:**
* **HOMO (Orbital 21):** A clear pi delocalization is observed over the aromatic ring, with density over the heteroatoms (the nitrogen of the amino group and the oxygen of the hydroxyl group). The presence of density on these heteroatoms indicates nucleophilic character.

* **LUMO (Orbital 22):** This orbital consists of an antibonding pi* system spread throughout the ring, characteristic of aromatic compounds. The multiple surfaces over the C-C bonds indicate its high energy and its unstable nature for electron retention.

**Reactivity Discussion:**
Based on the electronic properties described above, the initial reactivity between acetic anhydride and aminophenol is governed by the transfer of electron density from the **HOMO of aminophenol** to the **LUMO of acetic anhydride**.

Aminophenol acts as the nucleophile. The HOMO shows that electron density is available on both nitrogen and oxygen. Since nitrogen is less electronegative than oxygen, its valence electrons are at a higher energy level, making the amino group the preferred attack site.

Acetic anhydride acts as the electrophile. The shape of its LUMO clearly shows that electron insertion is located over the carbonyl carbons. Therefore, the initial reaction event involves overlap of the HOMO lobe on the aminophenol nitrogen with the large LUMO lobe on the carbonyl carbon of acetic anhydride. This overlap promotes charge transfer, initiates formation of a new C-N bond, and simultaneously forces cleavage of the C-O bond.


# 5. Reaction Mechanism and Intermediate Steps

<img src="images/mecanismo_de_reaccion.png"/>

*Figure 5: Reaction mechanism for the synthesis of acetaminophen.*

The mechanism shown in the figure corresponds to a typical **Nucleophilic Acyl Substitution**.
1. **Nucleophilic Attack:** The nitrogen atom of the amino group in p-aminophenol, using its lone pair of electrons (whose high energy was identified in the HOMO analysis), attacks the carbonyl carbon of acetic anhydride.
2. **Intermediate Formation:** This collision breaks the carbonyl pi bond, pushing electron density toward oxygen and generating an unstable intermediate. In this transient state, the attacked carbon changes hybridization from sp2 to sp3. The nitrogen temporarily acquires a positive formal charge by holding four bonds, while the pushed oxygen becomes negatively charged.
3. **Elimination and Proton Transfer:** The high electron density on the negatively charged oxygen is used to restore the carbonyl double bond (C=O). This forces cleavage of the adjacent C-O bond, expelling the acetate ion. At the same time, the nitrogen transfers its extra proton (H+) to the acetate ion, neutralizing both species and giving the final products: acetaminophen and acetic acid.

## Script Improvement: `script_thermo_xtb_complete_all.py` vs `new_thermo.py`

The main differences and advantages are as follows:

#### 1. Correction of Thermodynamic and Mathematical Errors

*   **Isolation of Zero-Point Energy (ZPE):** In the original script, ZPE was added multiple times in a redundant way (to the enthalpy and then twice more in the Gibbs calculation), which led to an overestimation of the final free energy. The new version computes and isolates ZPE, adding it only once using the formal equations: $H_{total} = SCF + H_{thermal} + ZPE$ and $G_{total} = H_{total} - TS$.
*   **Universal Physical Constants:** The incorrect frequency-to-kilocalorie conversion factor (`0.0002909`) was removed. The explicit physical constant $hc/k_B$ (`1.4388`) is now used, correcting the magnitude error.

*   **Correct Enthalpy Calculation ($H$):** The previous script assigned translational enthalpy as $3/2 RT$, confusing it with internal energy ($U$). The refactor now rigorously adds all internal energies and then applies the overall thermal correction by adding the $RT$ term.
*   **Rotational Degrees of Freedom:** The entropic contributions for linear and non-linear molecules were adjusted to reflect ideal-gas kinetic theory.

### 2. Vectorization with NumPy and Stability

The *for* loops were replaced with vectorized NumPy matrix operations. This allows all vibrational modes to be evaluated simultaneously and speeds up execution.

*   **Stability in Logarithmic Evaluations:** A mathematical safeguard (`+ 1e-12`) was added inside the vibrational entropy calculation using `np.log`. This prevents sudden script crashes or type errors when evaluating logarithms of zero at very low temperatures or very high frequencies.
*   **Dynamic Command-Line Interface (CLI):** Molecular linearity is no longer fixed as a default inside the file (`is_linear_molecule=True`). It is now controlled from the terminal (`--linear`), making the script much more flexible.

## C. Detailed Discussion of the Thermodynamics Involved

### 1. Spontaneity and Heat of Reaction ($\Delta G$ and $\Delta H$)
We can evaluate the overall thermodynamics of the synthesis step using the data at **298.15 K in water** for the reaction:
**4-Aminophenol + Acetic Anhydride -> Acetaminophen + Acetic Acid**

*   **Reactant Enthalpy:** $(-633.372) + (-645.974) = -1279.346$ eV
*   **Product Enthalpy:** $(-888.038) + (-392.047) = -1280.085$ eV
*   **$\Delta H_{reaction}$** = $-1280.085 - (-1279.346) = \mathbf{-0.739}$ eV

The negative enthalpy value ($\Delta H < 0$) shows that the reaction is **exothermic**. The system releases energy as heat when it forms the more stable amide bond, which matches the experimental reports from the Royal Society of Chemistry that the reaction vessel warms up.

Calculating the Gibbs energy of the system:
*   **Reactant Gibbs Energy:** $(-633.642) + (-646.396) = -1280.038$ eV
*   **Product Gibbs Energy:** $(-888.527) + (-392.256) = -1280.783$ eV
*   **$\Delta G_{reaction}$** = $-1280.783 - (-1280.038) = \mathbf{-0.745}$ eV (approximately **-17.17 kcal/mol**)

The strongly negative Gibbs energy ($\Delta G \ll 0$) confirms that acetaminophen synthesis is **highly spontaneous and thermodynamically favorable** at room temperature.

### 2. Solvation Energy (Water vs Gas Environment)
Comparing the tables shows why water is a good solvent for this reaction. For **acetaminophen at 298.15 K**, G_tot in the gas phase is -888.068 eV, while in water it decreases to -888.527 eV. This difference of **-0.459 eV** corresponds to the **solvation energy**. The ALPB model shows that water molecules polarize and stabilize the functional groups (-OH and -NH-CO-) through hydrogen bonding, lowering the free energy of the system. All reaction components (reactants and products) are slightly more stable in water than in the gas phase.

### 3. Temperature Effects
If the reaction is exothermic and spontaneous at 25 C, why heat the system to reflux (100 C / 373.15 K)?
From Table 6.3, entropy (S_tot) increases at 373.15 K because of thermal motion. This makes the Gibbs energies even more negative (G = H - TS).

However, heating is not done for thermodynamic reasons but for **chemical kinetics**. Applying heat at 373.15 K provides the activation energy needed to accelerate effective collisions between aminophenol and acetic anhydride, reducing the reaction time from 30 minutes (room temperature) to only 15 minutes (reflux).

### 4. Stability of the Intermediate Molecule
The calculations show an interesting observation: the intermediate in water has a G_tot of **-1280.863 eV**, which is lower (more stable) than the sum of the separated products (-1280.783 eV). Computation suggests that the intermediate is stabilized. In reality, cleavage of the C-O bond to eliminate acetate is driven by the gain in entropy.



# 7. Conclusions

## A. The value of computational modeling
Computational chemistry has proven to be an excellent tool for understanding the microscopic events that govern acetaminophen synthesis. Its main strength is making the invisible visible:
*   **Mechanistic support:** The nucleophilic substitution mechanism was validated by visually and numerically observing how the HOMO (on the nucleophile) and the LUMO (on the electrophile) determine the exact reaction pathway.
*   **Thermodynamic prediction and quantification:** The model showed that the reaction has $\Delta H < 0$ and $\Delta G < 0$, mathematically explaining the macroscopic observations reported in the laboratory (heat release and reaction spontaneity).
*   **Environmental analysis:** By simulating the system in gas phase and in water, solvation energy could be isolated and quantified, clearly showing why water is an ideal solvent that stabilizes polar species and charged intermediates along the synthesis route.

## B. Strengths and limitations of xTB
**Strengths in this specific and general case:**
*   **Computational efficiency:** xTB (specifically the GFN2-xTB parametrization) made it possible to perform geometry optimizations, vibrational frequency calculations, and molecular orbital extraction for medium-sized systems in very short time. Achieving this with ab initio or traditional DFT methods would require much more time and resources.
*   **Robust solvation model:** The ALPB model was able to capture solvent stabilization of the reactants, producing logical and meaningful changes in the infrared spectra and thermodynamic potentials.

**Limitations:**
*   **Semi-empirical approximations:** Because the method relies on tight-binding approximations and fitted parameters, absolute energy values may deviate from higher-level theory. For example, the calculated stability of the intermediate molecule (G_tot = -1280.863 eV) relative to the separated products (G_tot = -1280.783 eV) could be an artifact of over-stabilization by the ALPB model for a strongly polarized transient state.
*   **Unscaled harmonic frequencies:** IR frequencies are calculated under the harmonic oscillator approximation. Because natural anharmonicity is not fully included and no scaling factors are applied, theoretical bands (such as bond stretching modes) usually appear shifted to higher wavenumbers compared with an experimental IR spectrum.
